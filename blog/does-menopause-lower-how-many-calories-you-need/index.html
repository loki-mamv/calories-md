Does Menopause Lower How Many Calories You Need?

The short answer: Yes, a little, but menopause itself is only part of the reason. Federal calorie tables drop by about 200 calories a day for women at age 51, and that drop is tied to age, not to menopause. Studies that follow women through the transition show a small fall in energy use, a loss of lean mass, and less physical activity. The larger change is where fat is stored.

Do calorie needs drop at menopause?

They drop at midlife, and menopause adds a small push. The Office on Women's Health (OWH) says older women usually need fewer calories for energy. It also says many women gain an average of 5 pounds after menopause.

OWH does not blame hormones alone. It says lower estrogen may play a role, but the gain may also come from a metabolism that slows with age. It adds that women lose muscle mass as they age, and that many eat less well and move less than they did when younger.

Timing makes these causes hard to separate. OWH puts the average age of menopause in the United States at 52, with a usual range of 45 to 58.

How much is menopause and how much is age?

The best direct evidence is small. A four-year study in the International Journal of Obesity followed 156 healthy women who started out premenopausal. A subset of 34 women spent 24 hours in a room calorimeter at the start and again at year 4.

  • Energy use over 24 hours, and during sleep, fell with age in all of the women.
  • Sleeping energy use fell by 7.9% in women who became postmenopausal, against 5.3% in women who stayed premenopausal.
  • Fat burning fell by 32% in the women who became postmenopausal. It did not change in the others.
  • Physical activity fell 2 years before menopause and stayed low.

So the menopause effect was real but modest: about 2.6 percentage points more than age alone, measured in a few dozen women. The fall in activity is a separate cost, and it started before the last period.

A much larger data set gives the wider view. A 2021 study in Science pooled doubly labeled water measurements from people aged 8 days to 95 years. After adjustment for fat-free mass, total daily energy use stayed stable from age 20 to 60 and then declined in older adults. That stable span covers the usual age of menopause. The result suggests that body size and lean mass explain most of what changes in midlife, not a sudden slowdown in the cells. The abstract does not report on menopause directly.

What happens to muscle and fat during the transition?

Body composition changes more than body weight does. The Study of Women's Health Across the Nation (SWAN) tracked 1,246 women with repeat body scans around the final menstrual period. The average age at that final period was 52.2 years.

  • Before the transition, fat mass grew by 1.0% a year, about 0.25 kg. During the transition it grew by 1.7% a year, about 0.45 kg.
  • Lean mass rose slightly before the transition. During it, lean mass fell by 0.2% a year, about 0.06 kg.
  • Both changes continued until about 2 years after the final period, then flattened.
  • Weight climbed at a steady rate before and during the transition, with no acceleration.

The authors concluded that faster fat gain and lean mass loss are tied to the menopause transition itself. The scale did not show it, because one change offset the other.

The lean mass loss is small in calorie terms. A loss of 0.06 kg a year is about 2 ounces. Our article on how many calories a pound of muscle burns explains why a change that size moves resting energy use very little.

Why does fat move to the belly?

Fat distribution is where menopause has its clearest effect. In the four-year calorimeter study, all of the women gained fat under the skin of the abdomen as they aged. Only the women who became postmenopausal had a significant rise in visceral fat, the deeper fat around the organs.

The authors wrote that middle-aged women gained subcutaneous abdominal fat with age, while menopause itself was associated with more total body fat and more visceral fat. This is why a waistline can change when weight has barely moved. OWH notes that weight gain can raise the risk of high blood pressure, high cholesterol, diabetes, heart attack and stroke.

What do the Dietary Guidelines calorie tables say?

The current edition is the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030, released in January 2026. It has no calorie table by age and activity. It says the calories you need depend on your age, sex, height, weight and level of physical activity. It also says some older adults need fewer calories but equal or greater amounts of key nutrients such as protein, vitamin B12, vitamin D and calcium.

The most recent federal table is in Appendix 2 of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020-2025. Its estimates use a reference woman who is 5 feet 4 inches tall and weighs 126 pounds:

  • Ages 46 to 50: 1,800 calories a day if sedentary, 2,000 if moderately active, 2,200 if active.
  • Ages 51 to 60: 1,600 if sedentary, 1,800 if moderately active, 2,200 if active.
  • Ages 61 and older: 1,600 if sedentary, 1,800 if moderately active, 2,000 if active.

Three points stand out. The table steps down by 200 calories at age 51 for sedentary and moderately active women. The estimate for active women does not drop until age 61. And the table uses age, sex and activity only. It has no entry for menopausal status, so a woman who reaches menopause at 46 and one who reaches it at 56 get the same numbers.

Activity matters more than the birthday. An active woman of 55 has an estimate 600 calories higher than a sedentary woman of the same age. For a number based on your own size, see how to calculate your TDEE.

What does the evidence say helps?

Strength training. A meta-analysis of 26 studies covered 745 postmenopausal and older women, aged 50 to 80, who did resistance training. Programs averaged 16 weeks and 3 sessions a week. Lean body mass rose by a small-to-moderate amount, regardless of age. Fat mass did not change significantly, so lifting alone is not a fat loss method. OWH advises muscle-strengthening exercise on two days each week, plus at least 2 hours and 30 minutes of moderate aerobic activity.

Protein. The 2025-2030 Dietary Guidelines set a protein goal of 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight a day. For the 126-pound reference woman, that works out to about 69 to 91 grams. In a Women's Health Initiative analysis of 8,298 postmenopausal women, higher protein intake was associated with a higher percentage of lean mass. Women who were also physically active had the highest lean mass. That study was cross-sectional, so it cannot prove that protein caused the difference. Our protein intake guide covers how to reach a target.

Daily movement. Activity fell before menopause in the calorimeter study, and it is the part of energy use you control most. The 600-calorie gap in the federal table between sedentary and active women is larger than any measured menopause effect.

Sleep. MedlinePlus lists trouble sleeping among the symptoms of menopause and says that a lack of sleep can lead to fatigue over time. Our sister site insomnia.md covers sleep problems.

The bottom line

Calorie needs do fall around menopause, by roughly 200 calories a day in federal estimates for women who are not highly active. Most of that reflects age, body composition and activity. Menopause itself adds a small extra fall in energy use and a clear shift of fat toward the abdomen. Strength training, enough protein and steady daily movement address the parts you can change. A large cut in calories is not what the evidence supports.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories should a 55-year-old woman eat?

The 2020-2025 Dietary Guidelines estimate 1,600 calories a day for a sedentary woman aged 51 to 60, 1,800 if she is moderately active, and 2,200 if she is active. These numbers use a reference woman who is 5 feet 4 inches tall and weighs 126 pounds. Your own needs depend on your height, weight and activity.

Does metabolism crash after menopause?

The evidence does not show a crash. In a four-year calorimeter study, sleeping energy use fell 7.9% in women who became postmenopausal and 5.3% in women of similar age who did not. A large doubly labeled water study found that energy use adjusted for fat-free mass stays stable from age 20 to 60.

Why is my waist bigger when my weight is the same?

Body composition can change while weight holds steady. In the SWAN study, fat gain sped up and lean mass fell during the menopause transition, but the rate of weight gain did not change. A separate study found that visceral fat rose significantly only in women who became postmenopausal.

How much weight do women gain after menopause?

The Office on Women's Health says many women gain an average of 5 pounds after menopause. It lists lower estrogen, a metabolism that slows with age, loss of muscle, and changes in eating and activity as possible causes. Not every woman gains weight.

How much protein do women need after menopause?

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030 set a goal of 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight a day. That is about 69 to 91 grams for a 126-pound woman. The same guidelines say older adults may need fewer calories but equal or greater amounts of protein. People with kidney disease should ask their doctor first.
