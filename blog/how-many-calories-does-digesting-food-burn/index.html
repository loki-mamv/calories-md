How Many Calories Does Your Body Burn Digesting Food?

September 23, 2026 6 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Digesting, absorbing and storing food uses about 10% of the calories you eat. On a 2,000 calorie day, that is roughly 200 calories. Protein costs the most to process and fat the least, so the mix on your plate moves the number a little. In practice, the difference between a "good" and "bad" TEF diet is usually well under 100 calories a day.

What share of your calories goes to digestion?

Researchers call this cost the thermic effect of food (TEF) or diet-induced thermogenesis. It covers the work of breaking food down, absorbing nutrients, and storing what you do not burn right away. It is the smallest of the three parts of daily energy use, after resting metabolism and movement. For how the three parts fit together, see our guide to calculating your TDEE.

A widely cited review by Klaas Westerterp in Nutrition & Metabolism puts the number at 5 to 15% of daily energy expenditure on a mixed diet. For healthy people eating a mixed diet in energy balance, the review settles on about 10% of intake over 24 hours. A National Institutes of Health team that measured 509 adults in a whole-room calorimeter found that fed thermogenesis accounted for about 10% of 24-hour energy expenditure, with energy intake as its main driver.

The effect is slow and spread out. In the chamber data Westerterp reviews, metabolism had not returned to baseline by lunch, 4 hours after breakfast. Overnight, it took about 8 hours after dinner to reach basal rate. So your body is "digesting" for much of the waking day.

Does protein burn more calories to digest than carbs or fat?

Yes. The same review lists theoretical costs for each nutrient:

  • Protein: 20 to 30% of its calories
  • Alcohol: 10 to 30%
  • Carbohydrate: 5 to 10%
  • Fat: 0 to 3%

These are ranges for single nutrients. Real meals mix all three, so the effect gets diluted. In a regression across 19 meal studies, each extra 1% of energy from protein raised the thermic effect by about 0.22%. Alcohol also sits high on that list, but that does not make it a diet aid. Its calories still count, and it tends to add to what you eat rather than replace it.

A controlled test shows the size of the gap. Eight healthy women spent two 36-hour stays in a respiration chamber. They ate either a high-protein, high-carbohydrate diet or a high-fat diet with the same calories. The thermic effect was 14.6% of intake on the first diet versus 10.5% on the high-fat diet (1,295 vs 931 kJ a day). That works out to roughly 87 extra calories a day. Total 24-hour energy use, though, was not significantly different between the two diets.

A 2016 meta-analysis of 27 crossover studies in Nutrients found the same direction. Meals high in protein or carbohydrate raised thermogenesis more than high-fat meals, although the effect was not always significant. Medium-chain triglycerides raised it more than ordinary long-chain fats.

Do whole foods take more energy to digest than processed foods?

One small study says they can. At Pomona College, Sadie Barr and Jonathan Wright fed 17 healthy adults (12 women, 5 men) two cheese sandwiches with the same calories. The whole-food version used multi-grain bread and cheddar. The processed version used white bread and a processed cheese product.

Over the next 5 to 6 hours, the whole-food meal raised energy use by 137 calories (19.9% of meal energy). The processed meal raised it by 73 calories (10.7%). Satiety ratings did not differ.

Read this with care. It was one meal pair, in mostly young, lean college students. The whole-food sandwich also had more protein (20% vs 15% of calories) and about three times the fiber, so the study cannot say processing alone caused the gap. Energy use was measured with hourly spot checks, not a chamber. The result is interesting, but it has not become a settled number. For the wider evidence on processed diets, see our ultra-processed food guide.

Does eating more often speed up your metabolism?

No. The thermic effect tracks how much you eat, not how often. The 2016 meta-analysis found that for every 100 kJ more energy in a meal, thermogenesis rose by 1.1 kJ per hour. It also found that one large meal produced a higher thermic effect than the same food split into small meals or snacks.

Over a whole day, the pattern washes out. A review in the British Journal of Nutrition looked at "nibbling" versus "gorging." Studies using whole-body calorimetry and doubly labelled water found no difference in 24-hour energy expenditure between the two patterns. With one exception, meal frequency also did not change weight loss on reduced-calorie diets. Eat on the schedule that helps you control intake. The digestion cost will follow the total.

Does age or body weight change the thermic effect of food?

Possibly, but only by a little. A Mayo Clinic analysis compared 123 older adults (aged 60 to 88) with 86 younger adults (aged 18 to 35) who had TEF measured. The thermic effect was about 1% lower in the older group, even after matching for lean mass and meal size.

The picture is not settled. A 2026 review in Current Obesity Reports found 14 studies comparing younger and older adults. Six found lower thermogenesis in older people and eight found no difference. Studies that measured for 240 minutes or less tended to find an age effect. Longer measurements did not.

Obesity shows a similar pattern. A review in Obesity Research identified 29 well-matched lean-versus-obese comparisons, and 22 reported a significantly lower thermic effect in people with obesity. The authors linked the drop to insulin resistance. In the NIH chamber study, fed thermogenesis as a share of intake fell with age and fasting glucose, and dropped above a BMI of about 29. Westerterp notes that measurement problems make these findings hard to compare across labs.

Does the thermic effect of food matter for weight loss?

It matters at the margins, not at the center. Three points follow from the data above:

  1. It shrinks when you eat less. TEF is a share of intake. Cut 500 calories and you also lose about 50 calories of digestion cost. This is small, but it is one reason real weight loss runs slower than simple math predicts (see metabolic adaptation).
  2. Protein helps more through hunger than through heat. Westerterp's review ties protein's thermic effect to satiety as its main role in weight control. A higher-protein diet may add tens of calories a day in digestion cost. Its larger value is fewer calories eaten and better muscle retention in a deficit.
  3. Negative-calorie foods do not exist. No food costs more to digest than it provides. At a 20 to 30% ceiling, even pure protein nets most of its calories.

If you are on an appetite-lowering medication, intake falls sharply, and TEF falls with it. The glp1.md site covers how those drugs change eating. The thermic effect is a rounding factor there too.

The bottom line

Your body spends about 10% of the calories you eat on digesting them, or around 200 calories on a 2,000 calorie day. Protein costs the most, fat the least, and whole foods may cost somewhat more than highly processed ones. Meal timing does not change the daily total. Age and obesity may lower it slightly. Treat TEF as a small bonus from eating protein and less processed food, not as a weight-loss tool.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is celery a negative-calorie food?

No. No food costs more energy to digest than it supplies. Even protein, the most expensive nutrient to process, costs only about 20 to 30% of its calories. Celery is very low in calories, which is its real advantage.

Should I count the thermic effect of food when I track calories?

No. TDEE formulas and activity multipliers already include it. Adding it again would double count about 10% of your intake. Track what you eat and let your weight trend guide adjustments.

How long after a meal does the thermic effect last?

It lasts for hours. In respiration chamber data, metabolism had not returned to its baseline 4 hours after breakfast. After dinner, it took about 8 hours to reach basal rate overnight.

Does MCT oil burn more calories than other fats?

A 2016 meta-analysis found meals with medium-chain triglycerides raised thermogenesis more than meals with ordinary long-chain fats. The extra burn is small. MCT oil is still pure fat and dense in calories.
