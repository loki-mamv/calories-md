How Many Calories Do You Actually Burn Walking 10,000 Steps?

Your watch says 10,000 steps and 520 calories. Your weight has not moved in three weeks. Both things can be true, and the reason is in how that number is built.

The calorie cost of walking is one of the few numbers in nutrition you can actually estimate yourself, from published energy-cost tables, without trusting any device. Here is the arithmetic, and the two adjustments that make the result honest.

The short answer

Walking 10,000 steps burns roughly 300 to 500 net calories for most adults. The gross figure, which is what a tracker displays, runs about 25% to 30% higher: roughly 375 to 660 calories across a normal range of body weights.

Body weight drives the number far more than pace does. A 130-pound adult and a 230-pound adult walking the identical 10,000 steps differ by more than 200 net calories. Nothing about technique or effort closes that gap.

How is the number actually calculated?

Energy cost is standardized in METs, or metabolic equivalents. The 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities, published in the Journal of Sport and Health Science, defines one MET as an activity's energy cost relative to a standardized resting metabolic rate of 3.5 mL of oxygen per kilogram per minute, which is approximately 1 kcal per kilogram of body weight per hour.

That definition gives you a usable formula:

calories per hour ≈ MET value × body weight in kilograms

The 2024 Compendium assigns level walking these values: 3.0 METs at 2.5 mph, 3.8 METs at 2.8 to 3.4 mph, 4.8 METs at 3.5 to 3.9 mph, and 5.5 METs at 4.0 to 4.4 mph.

The remaining input is time. A cadence of about 100 steps per minute is the widely used threshold for moderate-intensity walking in adults, which puts 10,000 steps at roughly 100 minutes, or a bit under 5 miles for an average stride.

What does 10,000 steps burn at your body weight?

Using 3.8 METs and 100 minutes of walking:

  • 130 lb: about 373 gross calories, about 275 net
  • 150 lb: about 431 gross, about 318 net
  • 170 lb: about 488 gross, about 360 net
  • 190 lb: about 546 gross, about 402 net
  • 210 lb: about 603 gross, about 445 net
  • 230 lb: about 661 gross, about 487 net

One caveat the Compendium authors state directly: the tables "can serve as a starting point for prescribing individual activities but do not reflect precise individual EE values." Age, sex, height, body composition, and fitness all shift resting metabolism, so treat these as a well-grounded estimate rather than a measurement.

Why net calories are lower than what your watch says

This is the adjustment almost nobody makes, and it is worth about a quarter of the total.

Sitting on the sofa for those same 100 minutes, you would still burn roughly 1 MET's worth of energy. Those calories were happening regardless of whether you walked. They already sit inside the maintenance calories in your TDEE calculation. Counting them again as "exercise burn" double-counts them.

Net burn subtracts that baseline. For walking at 3.8 METs, the net value is 2.8 METs, not 3.8, which is why the net column above is roughly 26% lower than the gross column.

If you use a calorie target from a TDEE calculator and then add back your watch's gross burn as extra food, you have inflated your allowance twice over. That single error explains a lot of stalled deficits.

Does walking faster burn more?

Yes, but the effect is smaller than intuition suggests, because a faster pace also finishes 10,000 steps sooner.

For a 150-pound adult covering the same 10,000 steps:

  • Slow (2.5 mph, ~118 minutes): about 400 gross, about 267 net
  • Moderate (3 mph, ~100 minutes): about 431 gross, about 318 net
  • Brisk (4 mph, ~87 minutes): about 542 gross, about 444 net

Going from a stroll to a brisk pace adds roughly 175 net calories and saves half an hour. That is a genuinely good trade, but it is not the doubling that intensity marketing implies.

Hills change the picture more than speed does. The standard ACSM walking equation, VO2 = (0.1 × speed) + (1.8 × speed × grade) + 3.5, prices vertical work steeply. At a 5% grade, the climbing term alone adds nearly as much oxygen cost as the horizontal walking itself. A route with real elevation beats the same distance on the flat by a wide margin.

How wrong is your tracker?

Systematically wrong, in a way that is well documented.

A Stanford University evaluation of seven consumer wrist-worn devices found heart rate error below 5% for most devices, while energy expenditure error ranged from about 27% to 93% depending on the device and activity. None of the devices tested reached an acceptable accuracy threshold for calories.

The step count itself is usually the reliable part. The calorie figure is a modeled estimate layered on top of assumptions about your resting metabolism, and those assumptions are where the error accumulates. Our fuller breakdown of how accurate calorie counts really are covers the same problem on food labels and menus.

Practical takeaway: use your tracker's steps, ignore its calories, and calculate the burn yourself from the table above.

What does this mean for weight loss?

A 300 to 500 net calorie daily burn is real and worth having. It is also about one large blended coffee, or a second helping at dinner. It disappears easily.

Two effects reliably erode it. People tend to eat somewhat more after exercise, often without noticing. And total daily movement partly self-regulates, so a long walk can be followed by more sitting for the rest of the day.

None of that argues against walking. It argues against treating the burn as a budget increase. The most reliable pattern is to set an intake target independently and let walking do its other work: cardiovascular health, glucose control, mood, and appetite regulation. If tracking every calorie is not sustainable for you, our guide to running a deficit without counting covers the structural approaches that work instead.

It is also worth separating the calorie question from the health question. The largest analysis of step counts and health outcomes found that most of the measurable benefit arrives well before 10,000 steps. Our sister site covers that evidence in how many steps a day you actually need.

A simple rule of thumb

If you want one number to carry around: about 21 net calories per 1,000 steps per 100 pounds of body weight, at a moderate pace. A 200-pound adult nets roughly 42 calories per 1,000 steps; a 140-pound adult nets roughly 29.

Or more simply, per 1,000 steps at a moderate pace: about 28 net calories at 130 pounds, 32 at 150, 36 at 170, 40 at 190, 44 at 210, and 49 at 230.

Multiply by your actual step count, not your goal. That is the honest number.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories does 10,000 steps burn?

For most adults walking at a moderate pace, 10,000 steps burns roughly 300 to 500 net calories. Using standard MET values, a 150-pound adult walking about 3 mph for 100 minutes burns about 431 gross calories, of which about 318 are net calories above resting metabolism. A 210-pound adult covering the same 10,000 steps burns about 603 gross and about 445 net. Body weight is the single biggest variable.

What is the difference between net and gross calories burned?

Gross calories are everything your body burned during the walk, including the calories you would have burned sitting still. Net calories subtract that resting baseline and represent the extra energy the walk actually cost you. Most fitness trackers and treadmill displays report gross calories, which is why they look about 25% to 30% higher than the number that matters for a calorie deficit.

Does walking faster burn more calories for the same 10,000 steps?

Yes, but less than people expect, because faster walking also finishes the 10,000 steps sooner. In the 2024 Adult Compendium of Physical Activities, walking at 2.8 to 3.4 mph is 3.8 METs while 4.0 to 4.4 mph is 5.5 METs. For a 150-pound adult, a slow 10,000 steps nets roughly 267 calories and a brisk 10,000 steps nets roughly 444. Faster is better, but the gap is around 150 to 200 calories, not double.

How accurate are fitness tracker calorie estimates?

Poor, and much worse than their heart rate accuracy. In a Stanford University evaluation of seven consumer wrist devices, heart rate error was under 5% for most devices, but energy expenditure error ranged from about 27% to 93% depending on the device and activity. No device tested met a reasonable accuracy threshold for calories burned.

Is 10,000 steps enough to lose weight on its own?

Usually not by itself. A 300 to 500 net calorie daily burn is meaningful, but it is roughly one large coffee drink or a modest second helping. Research consistently shows that people partly compensate for exercise with increased intake and reduced non-exercise movement. Walking works best for weight management when paired with a deliberate intake target rather than treated as permission to eat more.
