How Many Calories Should You Cut to Lose One Pound a Week?

The short answer

The textbook answer is a deficit of about 500 calories a day. One pound of body fat stores roughly 3,500 calories, and 500 times 7 days equals 3,500. Public health guidance still uses this arithmetic: the CDC's gradual weight loss guidance describes 1 to 2 pounds a week as the steady pace people are most likely to keep off, and the HHS MyHealthfinder "Aim for a Healthy Weight" page puts the daily cut at 500 to 750 calories.

The catch: that rule is honest for the first month or two, then it overpredicts. A fixed 500-calorie cut does not keep producing a pound a week forever, because a lighter body burns fewer calories and the deficit quietly shrinks. So the practical answer has two parts. Start at 500 a day below your true maintenance, and expect to recalculate as you lose.

Where does the 3,500-calorie rule come from?

The number traces to a single 1958 paper by physician Max Wishnofsky, who estimated that a pound of adipose tissue stores about 3,500 calories of usable energy. As an estimate of the energy in fat tissue, it is reasonable. Fat tissue is mostly lipid, and lipid carries about 9 calories per gram.

The problem is what happened next: the estimate hardened into a prediction. Divide any calorie deficit by 3,500 and you supposedly get pounds lost, forever, in a straight line. That version has appeared in textbooks, patient handouts, and calculator apps for decades, and researchers at NIH have called it out directly in a letter titled "Why is the 3500 kcal per pound weight loss rule wrong?" in the International Journal of Obesity.

Two things break the straight line. First, weight loss is not pure fat. Early loss includes water and glycogen, and ongoing loss includes some lean tissue, each with a different energy content per pound. Second, and more important, the rule treats your calorie burn as a constant when it is a moving target.

Why does the 500-a-day rule overestimate?

Every pound you lose lowers your total daily energy expenditure a little. A smaller body costs less to run at rest and less to move around. On top of that, metabolism adapts to sustained restriction somewhat more than body size alone predicts, an effect we cover in depth in our guide to metabolic adaptation.

NIH researchers Kevin Hall and Carson Chow modeled this dynamic energy balance and replaced the 3,500 rule with a more accurate one. In the model behind their work, published as "Quantification of the effect of energy imbalance on bodyweight" in The Lancet (2011):

Every permanent 10-calorie-per-day cut in intake leads to an eventual loss of about one pound, with roughly half of that loss arriving in the first year and about 95% within three years.

Run the numbers on a 500-calorie daily cut held constant. The eventual result is a loss of about 50 pounds, but only about half of it, roughly 25 pounds, shows up in the first year. The old static rule predicts 52 pounds in year one and no plateau ever. The plateau is not a failure of willpower; it is the deficit closing as expenditure falls to meet intake.

This is why the honest framing of "500 a day for a pound a week" is: true at the start, then gradually less true. The NIH Body Weight Planner, built on Hall's model, exists precisely to replace the static arithmetic. Give it your weight, age, sex, activity level, and goal date, and it returns a calorie target that accounts for the slowdown.

How do you set the deficit from your own numbers?

A deficit is only meaningful relative to your maintenance calories, so that number comes first. Estimate your total daily energy expenditure using the steps in our guide to calculating your TDEE, or use the NIH planner above.

Then subtract. If your maintenance is 2,300 calories, a one-pound-a-week starting pace means eating about 1,800. If maintenance is 2,800, the target is about 2,300. For scale, the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2020–2025 estimate maintenance needs of roughly 1,600 to 2,400 calories a day for adult women and 2,000 to 3,000 for adult men, depending on age and activity.

Two rules keep the estimate honest:

  • Subtract from maintenance, not from what you currently eat. If you have been overeating, the gap between current intake and your target may be larger than 500, and that is fine.
  • Recalculate every 10 to 15 pounds. Your maintenance number at 190 pounds is not your maintenance number at 175. Refreshing the target restores the deficit the scale has been eroding.

How low is too low?

CDC materials describe cuts of 500 to 1,000 calories a day for a pace of 1 to 2 pounds a week. The upper end of that range is not right for everyone. For a smaller or less active person whose maintenance is near 1,800, a 1,000-calorie cut leaves too little room to meet protein, vitamin, and mineral needs, and steep deficits reliably cost more muscle and are harder to stick with.

NHLBI's heart-healthy weight guidance frames the goal differently, and usefully: aim to lose 5% to 10% of your starting weight over about 6 months. For a 200-pound adult that is 10 to 20 pounds, which a moderate deficit delivers without heroics. Faster than 2 pounds a week, or any plan built around very low intakes, is territory for a conversation with a healthcare provider first.

It is also worth naming a modern wrinkle: people taking GLP-1 medications often land in large deficits without counting anything, because the drugs suppress appetite directly. If that describes you, the sizing logic here still applies, but the guardrails matter more; our sister site glp1.md covers nutrition and dosing questions specific to those medications.

What does a 500-calorie deficit look like in practice?

Five hundred calories is smaller than it sounds on a menu and larger than it sounds in an app. A few honest equivalents: a large blended coffee drink, a bagel with cream cheese plus a soda, or roughly the net burn from walking 10,000 to 15,000 steps at most body weights, per our breakdown of calories burned walking 10,000 steps.

The deficit does not have to come entirely from food. A common split that survives real life: 300 to 400 calories trimmed from intake and 100 to 200 added through movement. And if logging every bite is not sustainable for you, structural approaches work too; our guide to losing weight without counting calories covers seven of them.

Finally, weigh in with the right expectations. The first week or two often overshoots a pound because of water and glycogen shifts. The pace then settles, and months later it slows below a pound a week even with perfect adherence. That slowdown is the physiology working exactly as the NIH model predicts, and the fix is a recalculation, not a crash.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a 500-calorie daily deficit enough to lose one pound a week?

At the start, yes, for most adults. A 500-calorie daily deficit is the standard prescription behind the 1-pound-a-week target, and public health guidance from the CDC describes cutting 500 to 1,000 calories a day to lose 1 to 2 pounds a week. Over months the same deficit produces less than a pound a week, because your calorie expenditure falls as your body weight falls.

How many calories are in a pound of body fat?

About 3,500 calories, an estimate published by Max Wishnofsky in 1958. That number for the energy stored in a pound of fat tissue is roughly right. The error is in how it gets used: real weight loss is not all fat, and the arithmetic ignores the fact that a smaller body burns fewer calories, so the rule overpredicts long-term loss.

Why did my weight loss slow down even though I kept the same deficit?

Because the deficit shrank underneath you. As you lose weight, your total daily energy expenditure drops, so the gap between what you burn and what you eat narrows even when intake stays fixed. NIH researchers modeled this dynamic effect and found that a permanent cut of 10 calories a day leads to an eventual loss of about one pound, with roughly half of that change arriving in the first year and 95% by about three years.

Is it safe to cut 1,000 calories a day?

A 1,000-calorie daily cut sits at the top of the range public health agencies describe for 1 to 2 pounds a week, but it is not right for everyone. It can push smaller or less active people below the intake needed to meet nutrient and protein needs, and larger deficits are harder to sustain and cost more muscle. NHLBI guidance frames a healthier goal as losing 5% to 10% of starting weight over about 6 months. Talk with a healthcare provider before making aggressive cuts.
