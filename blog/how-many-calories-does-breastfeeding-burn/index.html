How Many Calories Does Breastfeeding Burn?

September 6, 2026 7 min read By thrive.md Editorial Team
Medical advisor: Agustin Arrieta, MD — Otolaryngologist (ENT), South Florida

The short answer: Making breast milk costs about 500 to 540 calories a day in the first 6 months if your baby is exclusively breastfed. Your body pays part of that bill from pregnancy fat stores, so U.S. guidelines only ask you to eat about 330 to 400 extra calories a day. The popular "breastfeeding burns 500 calories" line is a rough average, not a personal number. Real milk output ranges from about 450 to 1,200 grams a day, so the calorie cost varies widely from one mother to the next.

Where does the 500 calorie figure come from?

The 500 number is the energy inside the milk itself. The 2023 Dietary Reference Intakes for Energy report from the National Academies measured the gross energy of human milk at an average of 0.67 kcal per gram. Women who exclusively breastfed produced about 808 grams a day. Multiply the two and you get roughly 541 calories of milk every day.

That is why the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) tells patients their body needs "about 450 to 500 extra calories a day to make breast milk."

Some estimates run higher because the body is not perfectly efficient at turning food into milk. The 1991 Institute of Medicine report Nutrition During Lactation put the energy demand at about 640 kcal a day above prepregnancy needs in the first 6 months. Butte and King (2005) estimated 2.62 MJ a day, or about 626 calories, after allowing for an 80 percent conversion efficiency. So "500" is the low end of the honest range. The full cost of making milk is closer to 500 to 640 calories a day.

Why does your milk output change the number?

Every figure above is an average. The calorie cost tracks how much milk you actually make. The Institute of Medicine's review of milk volume found intakes average 750 to 800 grams a day in the first 4 to 5 months, with a range of about 450 to 1,200 grams a day. At 0.67 kcal per gram, that range works out to roughly 300 to 800 calories a day.

Output falls once solids start. In the same review, milk intake averaged 769 grams a day at 6 months, 637 grams at 9 months, and 445 grams at 12 months. The 2023 DRI report uses 570 grams a day for months 7 to 12, which is about 380 calories of milk a day.

Three things push your personal number down:

  • Combination feeding with formula. The CDC notes calorie needs differ for mothers who exclusively breastfeed versus those who combine breast and formula feeding.
  • A baby who eats a lot of solid food.
  • Fewer nursing or pumping sessions per day.

How many extra calories should you eat while breastfeeding?

The CDC recommends "an additional 330 to 400 kilocalories (kcal) per day" for well-nourished breastfeeding mothers. That is less than the 500 or more calories milk costs. The gap is deliberate.

The DRI committee assumes exclusively breastfeeding women lose about 0.64 kg (1.4 lb) a month in the first 6 months. That gradual loss supplies about 137 calories a day from body fat. Milk costs roughly 541 calories, minus 137 from stores, leaves a net dietary need of about 404 calories a day. From months 7 to 12, the report assumes weight is stable, so the full 380 calories of milk comes from food.

ACOG frames it differently. It counts the full 450 to 500 calories and says a woman at a normal weight needs about 2,500 total calories a day. Both are correct. ACOG describes what milk costs. The CDC and DRI figures describe what to eat if you also want to lose pregnancy weight slowly.

A worked example: if your maintenance intake before pregnancy was 2,000 calories, the CDC range puts you at about 2,330 to 2,400 a day while exclusively breastfeeding. Our guide on how to calculate TDEE shows how to find your own baseline first.

Does breastfeeding help you lose the baby weight?

Less than most people expect. A 2014 systematic review in the International Journal of Obesity examined 45 studies. About 63 percent reported little or no association between breastfeeding and weight change. Among the 5 studies rated high quality, 4 did find a positive association. The authors concluded that the evidence "challenges the widely held belief that breastfeeding promotes weight loss."

The math explains the gap. A 500 calorie daily cost sounds like 1 pound of fat a week. But appetite rises with milk production, and the DRI expects only 0.64 kg a month of loss, about 3.8 kg (8.5 lb) over 6 months. If the scale is not moving, our article on gaining weight in a calorie deficit covers the usual reasons.

Structured programs do work. A 2013 Cochrane review of 12 trials with 910 postpartum women found diet alone produced an extra 1.70 kg of loss versus usual care, and diet plus exercise produced 1.93 kg. Exercise alone did not lead to significantly more weight loss. The interventions "seemed not to affect breastfeeding performance adversely."

Can you cut calories safely while breastfeeding?

Moderate restriction appears safe for the baby. In a randomized trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine, 40 overweight women who exclusively breastfed followed a diet and exercise plan from 4 to 14 weeks postpartum. The intervention group lost 4.8 kg over 10 weeks, about 0.5 kg (1.1 lb) a week, versus 0.8 kg in the control group. The infants' gains in weight and length were not significantly different between groups.

A companion 2014 review in Birth looked at 6 weight management studies in breastfeeding women. Diet-based interventions were the most effective at producing weight loss.

There is a floor, and it comes from nutrients, not milk supply. The Institute of Medicine report modeled intakes at 1,800 calories a day. At that level, predicted intakes fell below the RDA for calcium, magnesium, zinc, riboflavin, folate, phosphorus, iron, thiamin, and vitamin B6. That analysis is the origin of the common clinical advice to stay at or above about 1,800 calories while nursing. The same report also expects "gradual weight loss" during lactation, so slow is the goal, not zero.

Practical rules that follow from this evidence:

  1. Wait until breastfeeding is established. The NEJM trial started at 4 weeks postpartum.
  2. Aim for about 0.5 kg (1 lb) a week or less. Our one pound a week guide shows what that deficit looks like.
  3. Do not plan to eat under roughly 1,800 calories a day without talking to your clinician.

What else changes in your diet while breastfeeding?

Calories are only part of it. The CDC lists several nutrients that need to go up during lactation: vitamin B12, folate, vitamin D, vitamin A, iodine, and choline. The targets are 290 micrograms of iodine and 550 milligrams of choline a day. The CDC also notes that diet alone "may not ensure adequate nutrition" and asks clinicians to decide with each mother whether a supplement is needed. If you are weighing options, supplements.md covers the evidence behind common products.

ACOG adds a few more specifics:

  • Eat fish and seafood 2 to 3 times a week, but skip high-mercury fish such as bigeye tuna, king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, and tilefish.
  • Your ob-gyn may recommend continuing your prenatal multivitamin.
  • Drink plenty of fluids, and drink more if your urine is dark yellow.
  • Moderate caffeine, about 200 milligrams a day, most likely will not affect your baby.
  • After a single alcoholic drink, wait at least 2 hours before you breastfeed.

The bottom line

Breastfeeding burns about 500 to 540 calories a day in the milk itself during exclusive nursing. That drops to roughly 380 calories a day after 6 months as solids take over. You do not need to eat all of it back. The CDC and the Dietary Reference Intakes suggest about 330 to 400 extra calories a day, which allows a slow loss of about 0.64 kg a month. Eating around 1,800 calories or more, losing no faster than about 0.5 kg a week, and keeping iodine, choline, and your prenatal vitamin in place is the evidence-based path.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does pumping burn the same calories as nursing?

The calorie cost comes from the milk you produce, not the method. Human milk holds about 0.67 kcal per gram. If you pump the same volume you would nurse, the energy cost is about the same.

How many calories does breastfeeding twins burn?

Roughly double the milk means roughly double the cost. Milk output for one baby averages about 750 to 800 grams a day, so two babies can push the milk energy toward 1,000 calories a day or more. Talk with your clinician about intake, because the standard 330 to 400 extra calorie advice is written for one baby.

Will eating fewer calories reduce my milk supply?

Moderate restriction did not hurt infant growth in a randomized trial where mothers lost about 0.5 kg a week. The concern at very low intakes, around or below 1,800 calories, is your own nutrient adequacy, not supply. Severe restriction or rapid loss has not been studied well, so avoid it.

Do I still burn extra calories once my baby eats solids?

Yes, but less. Milk intake falls from about 769 grams a day at 6 months to about 445 grams at 12 months. The Dietary Reference Intakes estimate about 380 calories a day of milk energy from months 7 to 12.

Why does ACOG say 450 to 500 extra calories but the CDC says 330 to 400?

ACOG quotes the full energy in the milk. The CDC and the Dietary Reference Intakes subtract about 137 calories a day that the body draws from pregnancy fat stores. Use the CDC number if you want slow weight loss, and the ACOG number if you want to hold your weight.
